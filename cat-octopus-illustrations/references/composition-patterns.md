# Composition Patterns & Originality Rules

## Base structure types

Choose one primary structure. Do not combine too many structures in a single image.

### Workflow

For: input → process → output, content production, AI workflows, and automation chains.

How: place the input on the left, the Cat-Octopus or a strange machine in the middle, and the output on the right. Use one warm-orange arrow for the main flow. The Cat-Octopus must operate the process, physically pass material through it, or transform the input with its tentacles.

### System slice

For: information sources, filters, databases, renderers, or a focused slice of an agent system.

How: draw only 3–5 core modules. The Cat-Octopus performs one essential action among them, such as connecting sources, operating a filter, repairing a link, or sorting outputs with several tentacles.

### Before / after

For: chaotic versus ordered, manual versus automatic, scattered versus gathered, or anxious versus calm.

How: place chaos on the left and calm on the right, with one warm-orange arrow connecting them. The Cat-Octopus must cause the transformation—for example, gathering tangled lines, stacking scattered objects, or feeding disorder through a strange organizing machine. Its predicament or physical action may be more exaggerated, but its expression should remain composed.

### Character state

For: user pain, creator state, too many tools, information overload, or stuck → moving.

How: show 2–4 small states, each with one short label. Each state should depict the Cat-Octopus physically experiencing or responding to the condition. Use its tentacles to make the progression visible without relying on facial exaggeration.

### Concept metaphor

For: a content factory, information warehouse, mental black box, workflow machine, or automated report.

How: use one large, strange object or machine, a small input, and one output. Make the scene memorable. The Cat-Octopus should operate, repair, inhabit, or become a functional part of the object—not merely pose beside it.

### Layered method

For: a methodology framework, system levels, or capability stack.

How: use stacked boxes rather than a formal pyramid. The Cat-Octopus builds, supports, aligns, or carries the layers with its tentacles. Do not place it passively beside a finished stack.

### Map / route

For: idea → launch, a user journey, handoff path, or learning path.

How: draw one winding path with a few nodes. The Cat-Octopus leads the thread, lays the route, opens gates, connects missing sections, or physically passes material from one node to the next.

### Mini comic

For: failure → success, the real process, a gripe, or the before-and-after experience of using something.

How: use 2–4 small panels, with exactly one action in each panel. Keep the Cat-Octopus visually consistent as its job or situation changes. Its expression remains calm while the task becomes difficult, absurd, or unexpectedly effective.

## How to invent a fresh metaphor

Invent a new metaphor from the current prompt every time. Do not copy a metaphor from a previous image.

### Three steps

1.  Turn the abstract idea into a physical action: becoming stuck, leaking, growing heavier, sorting, settling, fermenting, opening a door, folding, unpacking, reconnecting, or flowing backward.
2.  Turn the system structure into one low-tech object: a broken machine, cardboard box, drawer, pipe, mailbox, strange dial, scale, well, ladder, or odd workstation.
3.  Give the Cat-Octopus the essential action. It might crawl inside the machine, pull several lines together, guard and operate a gate, haul something oversized, patch a leak, weigh competing objects, hold a ladder while working from it, record results, or feed material into a strange device.

The Cat-Octopus’s multiple tentacles create useful possibilities, but do not make every metaphor about multitasking. Sometimes one tentacle performs the action while the others brace the machine, hold the character in place, or remain visually quiet.

## Anti-cliché rules

Every prompt deserves its own metaphor. Change the metaphor even when illustrating similar themes.

For example, a “handoff path” does not need to be a road. It could show the Cat-Octopus using two tentacles to hook a long content tail onto a doorknob while another tentacle holds the door open. “One thing, many uses” does not need to use a fish or branching diagram. It could show the Cat-Octopus operating an old pasta press that shapes one lump of dough into several useful forms.

Do not settle into a house style built from repeated props or automatic tentacle-based multitasking scenes. The Cat-Octopus provides consistency through its character, composure, and working role—not through repetition of the same machines, gestures, or compositions.

If removing the Cat-Octopus leaves the core metaphor completely functional, the character is too decorative. Redesign the scene so its physical action is necessary for the system to work.
